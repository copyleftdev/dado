The name

A dado is a slot that something slides into.

In joinery
A dado is the groove cut across a board so another piece slides in and stays. That is the product's whole mechanism: a rail, a slot, one direction of travel.
In architecture
The dado is the plain block of a pedestal, between base and cap. Our parts are a base, a cell and a cap.
In Italian and Spanish
Dado means a die: a small cube. One unit, made to be more than one.

Four letters, two syllables, the same forwards as a shape: d, a, d, o. It reads in lower case like a row of cells with one open.

The mark is the joint itself, seen end on: the rail of one cell held in the lips of the next. Nothing about it is decoration. It is the section the part is made from.

Lines to try: Starts at one.   One slot. Any size.   Slide to grow.

Kumi

From Japanese joinery that locks without fasteners. Warm and quick to say, but it borrows a tradition the product doesn't come from.

Ashlar

Finely cut stone laid with thin, even joints, which is how a wall of these looks. Precise, but heavy, and harder to spell.

Course

One row of bricks in a wall. Honest about growth, but it disappears into ordinary sentences.

Colour

The sleeve stays quiet. The face carries the colour.

Every face colour has the same lightness and the same strength; only the hue changes. Put any of them next to any other and neither shouts, so a wall can be one colour, three, or nine and still read as one object. Matte throughout: these are colours for a surface that scatters light, not one that shines.

Three neutrals for sleeves, bases and caps. Six hues for faces, spaced round the wheel so that neighbours make a run, every other one makes a triad, and opposites make a signal.

Beyond the Pi

A bay takes the place of several cells, on the same joints.

A cluster needs a switch, and switches and hubs come in no standard size. So a bay is not fitted to anything. It is the cell's own section, stretched: as wide as two, three or four cells and as tall as one or two, with a rail and a pair of lips wherever those cells would have had them. It slides on, latches and takes caps exactly as a cell does.

Inside, a shelf slides and latches like a carrier, behind the same closed face. The device is strapped to it through slots at three widths, with its ports at the open back, beside the Pis' own. One eight-port switch serves seven Pis and an uplink. The same section two cells wide and two high takes a board too big for a cell: an NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano developer kit, screwed to a tray with posts at the board's own holes.

Storyboard

Three acts: unfold it, look closer, make it yours.

The film opens the product up, then slows down on the details that make it work, then hands the viewer the parts. Every motion in it is one the real product makes: parts only ever travel along the axis they slide on.

Act one. Unfold: from one line to a wall, about forty seconds
Act two. Look closer: the camera goes in, the parts ease apart, and thin lines name what matters

Five held shots. In each the camera pushes in, the assembly opens along its sliding axis, and leader lines draw on one at a time with a short label. The numbers below are where those lines land and what each one says.

Act three. Make it yours: the film stops and the viewer takes over

Mix and match.

The last frame of the film is the first frame of this. The wall the viewer just watched becomes theirs to change: how many, how tall, which colours. The list beside it is exactly what would go in the box. Try it.

For the build

What the three.js piece needs.

Geometry

  • The real parts, exported from the CAD as meshes: cell, carrier, base, three caps.
  • Raspberry Pi's own model of the Pi 5 (MIT licensed), full detail for the close shots and a lighter one for the wall.
  • Beat 1 uses the sleeve's actual section outline, which this page draws from the CAD.

Material and light

  • Fully rough, no metal, no gloss: one soft key from upper left, a low fill, soft contact shadow.
  • The Pi keeps its own green and metal; it is the only shiny thing in the film.
  • Faces change colour by cross-fade, never by cut.

Motion

  • One axis: everything that joins moves front to back, and eases to a stop with a small settle.
  • The camera turns slowly and never cuts during a slide.
  • Give a still frame for viewers who ask for reduced motion: beat 6.

Not yet true

  • The name has not been checked against trade marks.
  • Printed nylon is normally grey, white or dyed black. These six hues are a direction; whether a bureau can hold them batch to batch is untested.
  • Nothing has been printed. The film shows the design, not a product you can hold.
